Spiking neural networks (SNNs) compute with sparse binary spikes and run on neuromorphic hardware at a fraction of the energy of dense networks, which makes them a natural choice for embodied agents.
Such agents see only part of their world, and a world model gives them memory by compressing past observations into a recurrent state.
We find that existing spiking world models forget within a few steps, even when their neurons have long time constants.
The cause is reset: membrane potentials are cleared at every environment step and dendrites at every somatic spike, so all memory has to pass through binary spikes at every step.
Causal interventions on a spiking world model confirm this \emph{reset amnesia} and show that long time constants also need a normalized write gain.
Building on these findings, we propose the \methodfull (\method), a spiking world model in which dendrites store memory and spikes communicate it.
Its persistent dendritic neurons keep integrator and resonant dendritic channels that spikes never reset, with retention times from 1 to 1000 environment steps.
The integrators hold what the agent has seen, and the resonators also record when.
A stateless soma reads the channels and sends a spike count, the only signal that passes between neurons.
On BSuite memory length, \method recalls a cue 101 steps back within the budget of R2I, while DreamerV3 fails beyond 51 steps.
On POPGym RepeatPrevious, it is the only model, spiking or not, that learns to name the card shown exactly 32 steps earlier within 2M environment steps, and its world model also recalls exact lags of 64 steps on a task held out from development.
The agent uses \numEnergyRatio$\times$ less arithmetic energy per step than DreamerV3.
These results show that spiking world models remember over long horizons once their memory lives in dendritic state that spikes do not reset.
\end{abstract}
